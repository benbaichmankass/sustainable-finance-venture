# SFV Research Plan: Collective Finance, Risk Intermediation, and Securitization Readiness

## Purpose

This workstream investigates which collective institutions can originate, monitor, aggregate, hedge, and service member-level risks well enough to support external risk-sharing and, eventually, investable securitized cash-flow pools.

The immediate focus is on coffee-sector collectives in three pilot settings:

- **LATAM:** Huila/Cauca, Colombia
- **Africa:** Jimma/Sidama, Ethiopia
- **Southeast Asia:** Dak Lak/Lam Dong, Vietnam

The broader framework is designed to apply to VSLAs, SACCOs, producer organizations, cooperative unions, mutual guarantee associations, and platform-enabled farmer networks.

---

## Core question

> **Which collective institutions can originate, monitor, aggregate, hedge, and service member-level risks well enough to support external risk-sharing and eventually investable, securitizable cash-flow pools?**

### Subquestions

1. What types of collectives exist, and in which sectors, geographies, and legal forms?
2. What financial and non-financial services do they provide members?
3. How do collectives finance those services today?
4. Which lenders, insurers, buyers, exporters, fintechs, asset managers, and development-finance actors support them?
5. Which risks remain unpriced, unhedged, poorly monitored, or inefficiently retained?
6. Which risks can SFV measure, price, absorb, reduce, or transfer uniquely?
7. What data, governance, legal rights, and servicing capacity would make a collective “securitization-ready”?

---

## Working hypothesis

Collectives may reduce information asymmetry and servicing costs through local knowledge, peer monitoring, aggregation, recurring transactions, marketing relationships, and payment controls. However, most external capital providers still underwrite them as opaque single borrowers rather than decomposing their member, climate, price, delivery, liquidity, governance, and collateral risks.

SFV’s potential role is therefore not only to provide lending capital. It is to build the **risk infrastructure** that makes these collective cash flows measurable, comparable, hedgeable, and financeable at scale.

## Collective typology

| Collective type | Core purpose | Typical member financial role | Relevance to SFV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Producer/marketing cooperative | Aggregate output, quality, processing, and sales | Harvest advances, input credit, payment-on-delivery, retained patronage | Strong fit for coffee receivables and forward-sale structures |
| Savings group / VSLA | Savings, internal loans, emergency support | Member-funded short-term loans and social fund | Strong local screening; small tickets; potential standardized portfolio layer |
| SACCO / credit union | Member savings and credit | Deposits, working-capital loans, asset loans, insurance links | Direct origination and servicing channel |
| Farmer producer organization | Representation, extension, group procurement, marketing | Often brokers or facilitates finance | Distribution, data, and aggregation layer |
| Cooperative union/federation | Aggregate multiple cooperatives | Bulk purchasing, export/processing finance, guarantees | Potential regional pooling and structured-finance counterparty |
| Mutual guarantee association | Shared guarantee for members | Guarantees, common reserves, peer screening | Natural first-loss and partial-guarantee mechanism |
| Platform-enabled network | Digitally aggregate farmers, buyers, lenders, and insurers | Payments, scoring, embedded credit, marketplace access | Potential data/servicing partner; data rights require care |

## Key market gaps

### Fragmented underwriting

Many lenders assess a collective as a single borrower. The research should test whether risk can be separated into measurable components:

- Member repayment/default risk
- Collective liquidity and governance risk
- Climate and yield risk
- Commodity price and local basis risk
- Delivery and side-selling risk
- Quality and volume risk
- Buyer/offtaker and payment-delay risk
- FX risk
- Asset performance and collateral-recovery risk
- Fraud, warehouse-control, and operational risk

### Seasonal liquidity mismatch

Coffee collectives often need liquidity to buy members’ crop and pay operating costs at harvest, but may not receive export proceeds until later. Map the timing mismatch, current bridge-finance sources, payment-control mechanisms, and cost of capital.

### Weak integration of credit and risk transfer

Credit, insurance, commodity hedging, buyer contracts, and reserve funds are often structured separately. Test integrated structures linking:

- Crop-cycle operating loans
- Climate/yield triggers
- Price floors, forwards, or options
- Delivery commitments and collection-account controls
- Buyer/export receivables
- Group reserves and risk-sharing layers

### Data not translated into external underwriting

Collectives may possess repayment history, delivery data, quality scores, savings records, attendance, agronomic data, and local knowledge. Map which data exist, who owns them, their frequency/quality, and whether they can be converted into auditable risk features.

### Lack of standardization

External investors need standardized definitions, eligibility rules, reporting, loss waterfalls, reserve policies, audit trails, and historical performance. Identify the minimum common data model and servicing standards required for pooling.

## SFV value hypotheses

### 1. Collective risk operating system

Provide a modular risk toolkit for collective partners:

- Member-level PD/LGD/EAD modelling
- Crop-calendar-aligned repayment design
- Asset cash-flow and collateral monitoring
- Climate/yield and commodity-price stress tests
- Forward-contract delivery-risk model
- Group concentration, liquidity, and governance scorecard

**Test:** Does this reduce external capital providers’ uncertainty sufficiently to improve tenor, pricing, collateral requirements, or approval rates?

### 2. Parametric resilience layer

Design a pre-agreed loss-absorption layer triggered by weather/yield indices or portfolio losses:

- Index cover for systemic climate events
- Collective reserve for expected losses
- SFV or partner-funded excess-of-loss layer for unexpected loss
- Reinsurance or external capital for remote tail losses

**Test:** Does structured protection lower expected tail loss and improve the bankability of operating-credit portfolios?

### 3. Forward-sale and receivables structure

Help collectives create controlled, verifiable future cash flows:

- Validate buyer/offtaker quality and contract terms
- Model expected volume, quality, basis, delivery shortfall, and FX
- Link buyer payments to controlled collection accounts
- Advance finance against eligible receivables or inventory
- Use price floors or forwards while modelling side-selling incentives

**Test:** Can verified sales contracts and cash-flow controls reduce dependence on hard collateral?

### 4. Standardized risk-sharing contracts

Prototype defined layers rather than assuming SFV funds all loans:

- Partial credit guarantees
- Portfolio excess-of-loss protection
- First-loss reserve facilities
- Mezzanine risk notes
- Parametric climate-linked cover
- Productive-asset collateral-value protection
- Data-quality and servicing-performance incentives

**Test:** Which risk layer is most attractive to originators and funders while preserving collective incentives?

### 5. Securitization-readiness service

Build the operational standards needed before any capital-markets transaction:

- Loan and receivable-tape standards
- Eligibility criteria
- Data-quality tests
- Concentration and correlation limits
- Reserve-account and waterfall rules
- Stress-test packs
- Investor reporting templates
- Independent validation and audit trail

**Test:** Which collective types can satisfy the minimum servicing, governance, and data thresholds for structured finance?

## Screening scorecard

Score each prospective collective from 1 to 5.

| Dimension | A score of 5 means |
|---|---|
| Cash-flow observability | Digitized sales, delivery, payment, and repayment records |
| Contractability | Credible offtake, delivery, collateral, and collection-account rights |
| Diversification | Many members, limited borrower concentration, multiple micro-regions/buyers |
| Governance | Audited accounts, transparent leadership, reliable member records |
| Loss-history availability | Multiple cycles of repayment, sales, and recovery data |
| Climate measurability | Clear crop calendar and usable weather/yield proxies |
| Price-risk controllability | Pricing formulas, buyer relationships, hedging or price-floor access |
| Servicing capacity | Capacity to originate, monitor, collect, reconcile, and report regularly |
| Regulatory feasibility | Clear ability to lend, pledge assets/receivables, insure, and transfer risk |
| Member alignment | Benefits are clear; protections against exclusion and over-indebtedness exist |

### Interpretation

- **40–50:** Candidate for structured-pilot design.
- **30–39:** Candidate for a limited pilot with technical assistance, data improvements, or guarantee support.
- **Below 30:** Research/technical-assistance prospect; not yet suitable for external risk transfer.

These cutoffs are provisional and should be tested against actual partner evidence.

## Research sequence

### Phase 1: Desk mapping

1. Build a longlist of collective types and finance/risk-management providers in Colombia, Ethiopia, and Vietnam.
2. Map coffee co-ops, unions, SACCOs, exporters, buyers, lenders, insurers, and agricultural-finance platforms.
3. Create source-backed entity and product records in the Market Map.
4. Identify 3–5 priority organizations per country for deeper research.

### Phase 2: Organizational deep dives

For selected organizations, document:

- Legal form, governance, member composition, and scale
- Services offered and member uptake
- Full cash-flow cycle: planting/input purchase, harvest, procurement, storage, processing, sale, buyer payment, member settlement
- Funding sources, pricing, collateral, covenants, and repayment controls
- Historical performance, delinquencies, losses, recoveries, and shocks
- Buyer contracts, warehouse arrangements, insurance, and price-risk management

### Phase 3: Risk and data audit

1. Identify every material risk in the member-to-buyer cash-flow chain.
2. Assign current risk ownership and residual risk holder.
3. Assess what data exist, at what granularity/frequency, who owns them, and whether consent permits use.
4. Score collectives using the securitization-readiness scorecard.
5. Identify the smallest data/operations intervention that materially improves underwriting quality.

### Phase 4: SFV product design

Select one narrow, testable risk layer per partner, for example:

- Climate-linked excess-of-loss cover for seasonal operating loans
- Partial guarantee for a cooperative’s member-loan portfolio
- Forward-sale/receivables-backed harvest-procurement facility
- Solar asset-finance protection combining DSCR monitoring and collateral-value coverage
- Warehouse/inventory-finance loss-sharing facility

Define the product’s trigger, attachment point, premium/pricing logic, exclusions, data requirements, servicing duties, and alignment mechanisms.

### Phase 5: Pilot-partnership design

Specify the minimum viable partner configuration:

```text
Collective or cooperative
+ Local lender/originator
+ Buyer/exporter/offtaker where relevant
+ Insurer/reinsurer or risk-capital provider
+ SFV risk/data/structuring layer
```

Determine who originates loans, services them, controls collections, bears first loss, provides the risk layer, and reports to capital providers.

### Phase 6: Portfolio transition

Only after individual collective structures are validated:

1. Standardize data fields, eligibility rules, and reporting.
2. Simulate multiple collectives per region.
3. Model climate correlation within regions and price correlation across regions.
4. Pool cash flows and loss distributions.
5. Test reserve, first-loss, mezzanine, and senior structures.
6. Evaluate whether a private securitization, warehouse facility, guarantee program, or managed note is the appropriate first external-capital structure.

## Pilot focus: coffee collectives

For the three initial coffee prototypes, treat each cooperative as both:

1. A **credit intermediary**: facilitating or managing member-level CapEx and OpEx loans.
2. A **marketing and receivables aggregator**: aggregating voluntary coffee delivery, collective sales, buyer contracts, and payment flows.

The initial prototype should prioritize a narrow structure:

> **Seasonal operating and/or harvest-procurement finance, supported by verifiable buyer contracts and controlled payment flows, with a defined climate-linked or portfolio-loss risk layer.**

Do not begin with a public-market securitization objective. Begin by validating whether the collective can generate reliable data, service the facility, control key cash flows, manage delivery incentives, and sustain acceptable loss performance.

## Decision gates

### Gate 1: Is the collective a viable operating partner?

Minimum evidence:

- Legitimate organization and governance
- Member/product relevance
- Sufficient transaction scale
- Willingness/capacity to share anonymized performance data
- Clear member benefit and safeguards

### Gate 2: Is there a financeable cash-flow structure?

Minimum evidence:

- Identifiable repayment or buyer-payment source
- Credible servicing and collection process
- Defined collateral, receivables, delivery, or payment-control rights
- Manageable seasonal liquidity mismatch

### Gate 3: Is there a priceable risk layer?

Minimum evidence:

- Observable exposure and loss trigger
- Plausible historical proxy or scenario calibration
- A willing risk holder or transfer counterparty
- Premium/cost compatible with the underlying economics

### Gate 4: Is the collective portfolio-ready?

Minimum evidence:

- Standard data tape and reporting
- Reliable eligibility rules
- Sufficient servicing controls
- Understood correlation and concentration exposures
- Clear legal and regulatory path for any proposed financing/risk-transfer structure

---

## Governance principles

- Separate **observed data** from **assumptions**, expert judgments, and scenario parameters.
- Avoid treating participation in a collective as proof of creditworthiness.
- Evaluate member impact: affordability, coercion, exclusion, asset repossession, and debt stress.
- Obtain appropriate consent and establish data rights before using member-level information.
- Retain transparent risk-sharing incentives: originator, collective, and external capital should each bear risks they can influence.
- Treat securitization as a possible later-stage financing tool, not an end in itself.
